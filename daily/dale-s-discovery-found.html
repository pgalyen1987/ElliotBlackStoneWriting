Dale's Discovery Found

September 25, 2026 · 2623 words

Dale Dunmore sipped his lukewarm coffee, surveying the cramped control room as he worked to troubleshoot the platform's chilled water system. The maintenance logs had indicated a minor leak on the east wing, and he'd been called in to inspect it before rotation. He nodded to Jerry from Vessels, who was hunched over his console, eyes fixed on a screen displaying the platform's real-time structural stress.

"Hey, Dale, you got a minute?" Jerry asked, not looking up.

"Just got to this leak," Dale replied, gesturing to the schematics on his tablet. "Can't have the whole east wing freezing up."

Jerry nodded sympathetically. "Yeah, don't wanna be stuck with that mess after rotation."

Dale's gaze drifted to the maps plastered on the walls, monitoring the platform's pressure gauges and temperature readings. The crew was busy with the last week of pre-rotation checks – inspecting equipment, updating schedules, and scrubbing every inch of the platform.

As he made his way to the east wing, Dale ducked into a small storage closet to grab a tool. Amidst the tangled wires and discarded parts, he spotted a folded jacket draped over a shelf. His own jacket, in fact – he remembered wearing it on that one particularly chilly night shift three weeks ago. He'd tossed it aside when he changed at lunch, but must have forgotten to take it down.

Dale's thoughts returned to the task at hand as he tucked the tool into his belt and headed out into the dimly lit passageway. The east wing's chill water system was a mess, but he'd fixed worse on this platform before...

Dale's footsteps echoed through the narrow passageway as he made his way to the east wing's water treatment room. The dim overhead lights cast long shadows across the walls, but Dale had worked this platform long enough to know every trick of light and shadow. As he turned a corner, he caught a glimpse out of the corner of his eye – a maintenance worker, dressed in the same standard-issue coveralls as everyone else, moving down the corridor ahead.

But it was a half-second off.

Dale's gaze followed the apparent trajectory of the worker, but when he looked up again, they were still several paces away. It wasn't until the sound of their footsteps echoed back that Dale realized what had happened: his perception lagging behind the reality. He shook his head, chuckling wryly to himself. He'd been working too many long hours, that's all.

The worker in question turned into the treatment room ahead, and Dale followed, exchanging a nod as he passed by. "Hey, need some help?" he asked, gesturing towards the maze of pipes and valves.

" Nah, just checking the sediment filters," the worker replied, eyes already on their task. "Thought I saw something weird on the monitoring system."

Dale watched for a moment as they worked, his gaze drifting around the room to take in the usual assortment of tools and equipment. His eye landed on a maintenance log clipboard, hung from a hook on the wall. Someone had written a note on it: "Check 3B water sampling points". Dale made a mental note to look into that later.

He turned back to the worker, who was now peering intently at a small screen attached to one of the pipes. "You find anything?"

The worker looked up, squinting slightly. "Just some weird reading on the flow meter. Probably just a glitch."

Dale nodded, already moving off to investigate the sediment filters himself. He worked in silence for a few minutes, his focus entirely on the task at hand. When he finally straightened up and wiped the sweat from his brow, he noticed the smell of hot dust wafting from one of the heating elements nearby.

Dale moved on to check the water levels in the tankers, his eyes scanning the gauges for any sign of imbalance. One of the workers, Jenkins, approached him as he was making his way back to the main console.

"Hey Dale, you find anything on those flow meters?" Jenkins asked.

Dale's mental note from earlier flashed back into memory. "Just some weird reading," he said. "Probably just a glitch."

Jenkins nodded. "Yeah, I thought that too. Must be something with the programming."

Dale scribbled a quick note on his clipboard to look into the software update schedule. As he finished writing, he noticed Jenkins lingering nearby, watching him with an intensity that made Dale shift uncomfortably.

"Can I get you to take a closer look at those meters?" Jenkins asked.

Dale smiled wryly and handed over his clipboard. "Nah, I'm good on it. You're the expert."

He turned back to his work, feeling a faint sense of unease under his skin. It was nothing more than fatigue from covering for someone else's shift, but still... As he checked the pressure gauges on one of the pipes, a familiar voice chimed in behind him.

"You find anything on those flow meters?"

Dale spun around to see Johnson, another facilities engineer, standing there with his arms crossed. He rubbed his temples, suddenly feeling like he was trapped in some sort of bizarre déjà vu.

"Uh, no," Dale said firmly. "Just a glitch."

Johnson nodded and said, "Must be something with the programming."

Dale's eyes flicked to the nearby coffee mug with its chip on the side facing him – it was an old habit from the previous shift that someone must have left behind. He pushed the thought aside and refocused on his work.

But the repetition still nagged at him, making him feel like he was stuck in a never-ending loop of conversation. His hand brushed against the mug as he turned to check the sediment filters again – same side chip, same awkwardly placed crema stain from years ago.

Dale reached for his phone, habitually checking for signal strength before dialing Jenkins' extension on the platform's internal comms system. But as he waited for the call to connect, a faint buzzing in his earlobe told him there was no signal. He frowned, thinking it might be a glitch in the system, but when he tried the external line to call the offshore operations center, nothing happened.

"Come on," Dale muttered, trying again with the same result. He got up from the console and walked over to the exit door, checking his watch for what felt like the tenth time that hour. The platform was due for rotation in three days, and he was eager to get off this shift. But something about the feeling of being trapped began to nag at him.

He pushed open the door to the exterior catwalk and took a deep breath of the crisp, cool air. It was almost imperceptible, but there was a change in temperature. The sky above was still a pale blue, with wisps of high-level cloud drifting across it. A faint breeze carried the smell of saltwater and something else – something earthy and slightly metallic.

As he scanned the horizon for signs of the platform's support vessels, Dale noticed the clock tower on the operations module's roof. It read 11:47 a.m., but something didn't feel right about the numbers. The hands seemed to be moving at an almost imperceptible crawl, as if time itself was slowing down.

Dale shook his head, trying to clear it. He glanced around at the platform's steel latticework and the rows of pipes snaking along its sides. Everything looked normal, but...

Dale's gaze drifted back to the clock tower, and he felt his eyes lock onto it like a magnet. The hands still crawled, but now they seemed to be... arguing. "Three-forty-seven," one hand whispered, while the other countered, "Eleven-fifty." Dale's mind recoiled at the absurdity of it. What was happening to his perception? He shook his head again, trying to shake off the feeling that he was trapped in some kind of malfunctioning time loop.

He turned away from the clock tower and began to walk towards the operations module's life support console. As he walked, he heard the sound of footsteps behind him. Jenkins' voice called out, "Hey, Dale! Just wanted to check on you." Dale slowed down, expecting to see his colleague's familiar face around the corner.

But when he turned, there was no one there. The sound of Jenkins' voice repeated itself from the previous time this conversation had played out: "Dale, what are you doing here?" This time, however, it was slightly different – the tone was a fraction sharper, the words themselves unchanged.

Dale's eyes scanned the platform's corridors, but he knew he was alone. The smell of hot dust wafted up from the life support console, and he felt his stomach twist with unease. He remembered the conversation playing out before – Jenkins' voice, repeating the same phrase with one word changed each time.

He couldn't explain it anymore. The explanation had worn thin, like a thread pulled taut until it snapped. Dale's eyes dropped to the floor as he watched his own feet carry him towards the operations module's life support console. It was gone – the exit, the escape route, the way back. He felt it like a cold draft on the back of his neck.

A photograph taped to the life support console caught his eye: Dale himself standing in front of the platform's control room, smiling at something off-camera. His wife, Rachel, stood slightly out of frame, her arm around him – but it was the angle that made him pause. She shouldn't be looking over her shoulder like that.

Dale's gaze snapped back to the life support console as he took a step closer, his heart thudding against his chest. The photograph still held his attention – Rachel's arm around him was a gesture of protection, but her angle made it seem like she was warding something off. He shook his head; it was just a trick of the camera.

The air in the operations module seemed to grow heavier, weighing on Dale's lungs as he drew a breath. Sweat beaded on his forehead and upper lip, and his skin prickled under the fine tremble of the platform's engines. A faint metallic tang, like ozone before a storm, tinged the musty smell of the console's metalwork.

A faint creaking echoed from the life support console itself – it sounded like the slow groan of a tired engine sputtering back to life. Dale's eyes darted to the readouts on the console's screen: pressure building in the module, temperature dropping, and oxygen levels dipping below safe limits. The readings were off; something was wrong with the system.

As he watched, a fine mist began to seep from the console's vents, tendrils of it curling around the edges of the photographs stuck on the surface. It looked like condensation, but Dale knew better – this stuff had an oily sheen that clung to skin and surfaces.

He swallowed hard, his mouth dry with fear as he took another step closer to the console. The photograph, now shrouded in mist, seemed to loom over him like a dark presence. Rachel's smile seemed to falter on the edge of the frame – or was it something else entirely? Dale's eyes were glued to the image as he felt his breathing slow, grow more labored.

A cold dread seeped into his bones, as if the platform itself had grown colder. His skin crawled under the fine tremble of the engines; every cell in his body screamed for him to move, to get away from this... thing. But Dale's feet seemed rooted to the spot, transfixed by the sight of Rachel – and whatever it was she was looking at with such a mix of fear and warning.

Dale's gaze snapped back to the monitor as his breath caught in his throat. Rachel's face was still frozen on the screen, but now her eyes were locked on his. The camera feed flickered once more before stabilizing into a clear view of the control room. Dale's mind reeled as he took in the scene: Jenkins slumped over the console, blood pooling around the keyboard; the usually fastidious engineer's hair was matted with grease and oil.

The camera continued to roll, but now it was moving on its own, panning across the room. The viewfinder wavered, and for an instant, Dale thought he saw a faint scribble on the wall behind Jenkins' head: "They're watching". Then the image steadied, and he was left staring at his own reflection in the monitor.

A chill spread through him as the camera continued to pan, coming to rest on the logbook beside his chair. The entries for the past week were neatly written, but one page stood out: dated 11:47 a.m., just now. Dale's name was scrawled across it, along with two words: "Parental Note". He felt his skin crawl as he read on.

The entry detailed how he had taken over Rachel's duties on the platform, ensuring her son's safety from... "outside threats". The words blurred together as Dale's mind reeled. Jenkins' slumped form came into focus again, and he noticed something that made his blood run cold: a small, makeshift notebook hidden behind Jenkins' ear.

The notebook's pages were filled with crude drawings of the platform's layout, but one symbol stood out: a crude image of a mother holding a child, the child's face a crude sketch of Dale. The realization hit him like a sledgehammer: this whole thing had been a ruse. Rachel was never in danger; Jenkins was never an engineer; and the "wrong details" were nothing more than a subtle nudge towards his own participation.

Dale's eyes locked onto the monitor, where Rachel's face still stared back at him. For an instant, he thought she mouthed something – but it was just a faint echo of what he already knew: that he had been playing along, unwittingly assuming responsibility for this twisted charade. The camera continued to roll, but now Dale saw it for what it truly was: a tool, a means of keeping him in the loop as he took over the platform's administration.

Dale's gaze drifted from the monitor, his mind reeling with the implications of what he'd discovered. He tried to shake off the feeling of unease that lingered, but it clung to him like a damp mist on a cold winter morning.

As he stepped out of the cramped control room, the bright lights of the platform's hub assaulted his eyes. People bustled about, going about their day as if nothing had changed. He felt a pang of disconnection from this new reality – one where he was complicit in a twisted game that threatened to consume everything.

Rachel's face still lingered on the monitor, and he knew he couldn't shake off the image anytime soon. He needs to file reports.

Dale took a deep breath and made his way to the facilities office, intent on reviewing the schedule for the upcoming rotation. He slid into his chair, running through the list of tasks assigned to him. The names stared back at him – Jenkins, Rachel, Jenkins' team... He noted down the corrections needed to the shift assignments.

The keyboard clacked under his fingers as he worked, the silence broken only by the hum of the air conditioning and the occasional bleep of a passing alarm. Hot dust coated his skin, sticking to the heating element behind the computer – a constant reminder that the platform's machinery still churned on, oblivious to the turmoil brewing within its core.

The clock ticked away: 12:14 p.m. The world moved on, unaware of the fragile balance that had been disrupted in those few minutes before. Someone should check the weather forecast for the incoming crew.
