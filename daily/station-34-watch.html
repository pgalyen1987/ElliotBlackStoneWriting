Station 34 Watch

September 22, 2026 · 2454 words

Franklin Ohara filled his thermos with tap water from the kitchen sink. He'd already made coffee, but the caffeine would wear off by lunchtime, and he needed something to keep him going during the afternoon watch. The burn ban had been in effect for three weeks now, but it was a dry spell even before that. The forest service needed someone to keep an eye on things from up here.

He grabbed his worn leather jacket from the hook and slung it over his shoulder, along with the binoculars and the radio headset. His eyes flicked to the clock: 10:04 AM. He'd been here since 9, but he liked to get settled before calling in for his morning report. The radio crackled to life as he picked up the mic, and his voice went out over the comms system: "Tower to dispatch, this is Franklin Ohara reporting for duty at Station 34."

As he walked into the main room, he nodded at the worn photograph on the wall. It was an old picture of the original crew that staffed the tower back in '62. He'd seen it a thousand times before, but it still gave him a sense of history. The faded nameplate on the edge of the photo caught his eye: "R. Jenkins." Franklin's gaze lingered for a moment, but he didn't think twice about it. Jenkins had worked here long ago; his replacement had come and gone. It was just a name etched into the past.

Franklin settled into the creaky chair behind the control panel, running through the familiar checks: firefinder radar, temperature gauge, wind direction. The dry air outside made every surface feel like it was coated in a fine layer of dust. He took a swig from his thermos and set it down beside him, the cup's metal lid clinking against the Formica table. His eyes drifted out to the panorama, but for now, nothing seemed amiss.

Franklin's gaze lingered on the control panel, his eyes scanning the rows of buttons and dials as he ran through the pre-shift checks. His mind began to wander, thinking about the latest updates from R. Jenkins' replacement, a young woman who'd been working here for just over a year now. She'd mentioned something about some minor adjustments to the firefinder's algorithms, but Franklin wasn't sure what that meant.

As he stood up to stretch, his eyes caught on a phone hanging from a hook on the wall. It was an old model, with a cordless receiver that looked like it hadn't been used in years. The phone rang suddenly, shrill and loud in the silence of the tower. Franklin's head jerked towards it, his heart skipping a beat.

But as he looked closer, he saw there was no line connected to the phone, just a tangled mess of wires and empty brackets. He felt a slight twinge of confusion – why would someone leave a phone up here with no way to use it? – but only for a moment.

"Probably just a leftover from when R. Jenkins worked here," Franklin told himself, shrugging off the unease. "Or maybe one of the maintenance guys must've hung it up as a joke." He chuckled to himself, feeling silly for getting spooked by an old phone.

He turned back to his work, running through the next set of checks and making a mental note to ask one of the maintenance guys about the phone later. As he sat down in the creaky chair, the silence of the tower returned, broken only by the soft hum of the equipment and... many people breathing quietly in an empty room.

Franklin made his way to the kitchenette, where he poured himself a cup of coffee from the pot that had been brewing since morning. As he added creamer and sugar, he noticed the mug in front of him was chipped on the left side - just like his favorite coffee mug back home. He must have grabbed it by mistake when he restocked the supplies. Shrugging, he thought about switching mugs, but decided to leave it as is.

He carried his coffee to the desk and began going through the day's logs, reviewing the previous shift's handovers with the forest rangers who patrolled the area. The silence of the tower was a comfortable one - the kind that comes from being alone in a space where sound would normally bounce off the metal and glass.

As he worked, Franklin became aware of a faint, repetitive noise coming from somewhere deeper in the tower. He couldn't quite place it - a low whine, maybe, or a gentle hum? He tried to tune it out, focusing on the reports and handovers, but the sound persisted, like a quiet murmur in the background.

He finished his coffee and wiped down the desk with a cloth, taking note of the 10:15 handover time. The rangers would be coming by soon to review the fire situation and assess any changes in the burn ban. Franklin's thoughts turned to his own schedule - he was due for a break at 11:00, when one of the maintenance guys would come by to swap out some filters.

The whine or hum continued to simmer just below the surface, its pitch steady but its volume fluctuating ever so slightly. Franklin tried to convince himself it was just some electrical quirk - probably something with the old tower's wiring. He pushed the thought aside and focused on his task, making mental notes for the handover report.

The routine continued, but the air in the tower seemed heavier now, charged with an almost imperceptible tension.

Franklin's gaze drifted towards the radio console as he checked the time – 10:23 now. He needed to confirm the updates on the burn ban with someone outside, just to be sure they were all on the same page. He keyed the mic, calling out to dispatch, but got only static in return.

He fiddled with the dials, trying to tweak the reception, but it seemed to be dead as a brick. Shrugging, he decided to take his concerns to someone in person. Grabbing his jacket, Franklin headed down from the tower, the wooden stairs creaking beneath his feet.

The air was warm and still, a peculiarly oppressive weight that made his skin prickle. He breathed in deeply, but the usual scent of pine and earth was masked by something...else. Something dry and acrid. He coughed, tasting the fine grit that coated the back of his throat.

Down at the base, Franklin noticed something odd – the main gate was closed, instead of being left open for easy access. A piece of twine held it shut, tied to a rusty hinge. He examined it more closely: no tag, just a length of frayed rope with a few well-worn knots. Not exactly standard procedure.

Franklin squinted into the sun, wondering if maybe it was a new policy or something he'd missed, but the twine looked weathered, not like it had been put up recently. He pondered for a moment before shrugging and pushing the gate open. The hinges groaned in protest as it swung wide.

But as Franklin walked out into the bright sunlight, he realized his phone was dead, the battery sucked dry. Great, just what he needed – to be cut off from the outside world at this precise moment. He cursed under his breath, tucking the phone away and heading back towards the tower.

Franklin's foot hit the worn wooden stairs hard, and he felt a jolt of pain shoot through his ankle. He cursed under his breath as he hopped on one foot, trying to shake off the numbness. Stupid old tower. He'd been moving around this place for two weeks now, and his feet still didn't seem to be getting used to it.

As he climbed, the sounds of the forest grew quieter – a few birds chirping here, a rustle of leaves there – but beneath that, Franklin swore he heard the quiet breathing. Like someone was watching him from just out of sight. He shook his head, chuckling wryly to himself get a grip, Ohara. It was just the wind, or maybe his imagination playing tricks on him.

He reached the top platform and took a moment to check the fire lookout's logbook, flipping through the pages until he found the latest entry. The handwriting was neat and precise – it looked like his replacement would be arriving in an hour to relieve him. Franklin made a mental note to leave everything tidy for the newcomer, then glanced at his watch: 11:04. Still on schedule.

He moved over to the small desk where the radio equipment sat, reaching for the phone to call his supervisor and confirm the handover. But as he picked it up, his fingers brushed against something else – a form, clipped to the edge of the desk with a sticky note that read "OHARA: PLEASE SIGN WHEN COMPLETE". Franklin's heart sank as he saw the title: "Incident Report – Fire Tower #17". He felt a cold sweat break out on his forehead.

What incident?

He looked down at the form, and for a moment, the quiet breathing in the forest seemed to grow louder. The words on the page blurred together until all Franklin could make out were the numbers: 13 steps, 4 paces, 8 feet by 6...

The air inside the tower seemed to thicken, making every breath Franklin took feel like he was inhaling a wet sock. Sweat dripped down his temples as the temperature rose – not drastically, but enough to make him notice the creak of the wooden stairs beneath his feet. The pressure in his head grew, a dull ache that pressed on his eyes.

Franklin's gaze snapped back to the form on the desk. 13 steps... He'd been counting them in his head since he'd first seen the number. Maybe it's just a maintenance thing, he thought, but the doubt gnawed at him like a mouse in the walls. He pushed it aside, telling himself to focus on getting this situation under control.

The breathing in the forest seemed louder now, and Franklin's own chest rose and fell with an almost imperceptible hesitation. The sound of his own lungs expanding and contracting was amplified in the stillness – a tiny drumbeat that made him feel like he was drowning. Don't panic, he thought, but it was too late.

A draft caught the edge of the form, sending it fluttering onto the floor. As Franklin bent to pick it up, his fingers brushed against something cold and smooth – metal, maybe – hidden beneath the wooden planks. The smell of rust wafted up, pungent and unexpected in the otherwise clean space.

The stairs beneath him groaned louder now, the wood creaking like an old man's joints as Franklin shifted his weight to stand up straight. What have I been standing on? He couldn't remember ever noticing the planks were uneven before. It doesn't matter, he thought, but it did. It mattered a lot.

Franklin's eyes drifted down to the floor as he picked up the form, his fingers closing around it like a lifeline. The paper felt strange in his hand, too thick and heavy for its size. He flipped it over, and his gaze snagged on a scribble at the bottom. A signature.

His own signature.

Franklin's brow furrowed as he took a step back, the form slipping from his grasp. It clattered onto the floor, and he stared down at it, his mind struggling to catch up. The handwriting was unmistakable – messy loops and swoops that seemed to writhe off the page like living things.

He tried to remember signing something, but his memories were hazy. Fragments of conversation floated in his head: a voice asking him questions, a pen gliding across the paper... but it didn't feel right. It felt like someone else's life he was recalling.

Franklin's eyes dropped to his own hand, flexing his fingers as if expecting something to fall off. The thought sent a shiver through him – not just because of the handwriting, but because of the faint tremble in his palm. He'd never been prone to shaky hands before this job, and yet...

He spotted it then: on the edge of the form, where he'd scribbled some illegible notes. A clock time, scribbled in red – 10:23. The same time as... something else. Franklin's mind latched onto it, his gaze snagging on a word he couldn't quite place.

The stairs creaked and groaned beneath him, the sound like a living thing trying to get his attention. Franklin's eyes snapped up to the window, where the morning light was beginning to seep through the gaps in the shutters. It was almost 11:04 now – a time he'd written down himself, somewhere.

He stumbled forward, grabbing for the phone on the wall. His fingers closed around it, and for a moment, all he could think about was calling for help. But something – the signature, the handwriting, the way his hand trembled – stopped him cold.

The routine returned. Franklin's phone call was never made. He stayed put, frozen by the thing on the wall. The fire lookout tower creaked and groaned with the wind, the wooden stairs shifting beneath his feet. Time passed.

At 11:14, the forest service's dispatcher at headquarters called the lookout station to check in. There was no answer. She jotted down a note: "Franklin Ohara, unresponsive." The phone call went to voicemail. Franklin's voice on it said nothing out of the ordinary – routine updates, weather reports, nothing that would raise an eyebrow.

At 12:05, the fire lookout team leader at headquarters tried again. This time, his call connected. A recorded message played back: "Hello, this is Franklin Ohara. I'm on duty at the... uh..." The message cut off, and silence followed. The team leader's note said simply: "Problem at station 7-A."

At 12:37, a maintenance crew arrived to check the station's equipment. They found nothing wrong with the radio or phone lines. A few loose screws, maybe, but that was it. Franklin Ohara's belongings were still in his room – clothes, books, a pack of cards on the bed.

The day went on. The sun beat down on the forest floor, casting long shadows through the trees. Fire crews moved through the woods, searching for hotspots. Back at headquarters, dispatchers juggled calls, updating maps and records.

In the silence of the empty room, Franklin Ohara's voice whispered one final time: "...I'm on duty..." *The breathing was quiet, steady – thirty-five people in a row, each one inhaling exactly as they had before. Thirty-four. Thirty-three.
