Cable Landing Station

September 13, 2026 · 1979 words

Cyrus Sandoval poured himself a lukewarm cup of coffee from the thermos on his truck's passenger seat, its surface etched with faint oil stains from countless sea salt deposits. He'd taken this night watchman job at the submarine cable landing station for the peace and quiet – a break from the constant din of city life after his wife left him. Now, in the stillness of 3:17 a.m., the Pacific's vast darkness pressed against the reinforced steel and concrete enclosures housing the cables that linked the continent to the rest of the world. A few scattered stars pricked the sky above.

He locked his truck, its wheels squeaking on the weathered asphalt, and made his way towards the main control building, a hulking structure of rust-resistant aluminum and corrugated steel. The exterior lights cast long shadows across the concrete, but Cyrus knew every inch of this station by heart – he'd been working here for six months now.

As he stepped into the airlock, the hiss of pressure equalization filled his ears. He passed through into the dimly lit control room, where banks of humming servers and LED displays glowed with a soft blue light. His eyes fell on the usual rows of screens: status indicators for the cables, ocean depth sensors, and an emergency generator's fuel level gauge. A key, labeled "D-9", hung from his ring alongside the others – keys for the generator room, the cable repair shack, and the airlock – but Cyrus had no idea what D-9 unlocked. He hadn't seen the corresponding lock on any of the station's doors or equipment cabinets during his six months here.

His gaze swept over the screens as he went about his routine check: verifying that all systems were online, monitoring the cable's signal strength, and checking for any unusual changes in ocean currents or sea temperature. The data flowed onto his tablet like a steady stream of water – numbers and graphs that made no sense to him, but which told him everything was normal, just as it should be.

Cyrus's gaze drifted from the screens to the small galley, where a steaming plate of eggs and bacon sat on the counter. His stomach growled at the sight, but he shook his head. He'd eaten an hour ago. Still, someone must have decided to leave him a snack, thinking he might get peckish later.

He shrugged and picked up the plate, examining it for any signs of tampering or recent preparation. The eggs looked freshly cracked, the bacon crisped just right. Whoever made this meal had done a decent job – almost as if they'd been trained in a kitchen. Cyrus set the plate back down on the counter, deciding to leave it there.

He checked the comms console for any messages from the owner's office or maintenance teams, but there was nothing new since his last check. The station's routines were well-established; everyone knew what needed doing and when. Cyrus's thoughts began to wander as he went through the motions of his rounds: would he have time to read that book he'd brought with him before his shift ended? Maybe grab a shower if he had an hour free?

The intercom crackled to life, and Cyrus's eyes snapped back to it. He frowned, expecting a message or perhaps some kind of announcement, but instead of a voice, the sound just kept going – static, like someone was holding the mic open, ready for him to respond.

Cyrus's eyes narrowed as he listened to the static-filled intercom, wondering if someone had left it open by mistake or if there was a problem with the comms system. He glanced around the dimly lit station, but everything seemed normal. The console in front of him flickered softly, casting an faint glow on the floor. He let out a low sigh and rubbed his eyes, feeling a creeping sense of fatigue.

Shrugging it off, he moved on to the next task: checking the landing gear's condition. As he walked towards the storage room, his foot landed on something familiar – the worn soles of his spare boots, already broken in from months of wear. He'd left them here after his last visit. Cyrus felt a flicker of relief; at least someone had been using the station recently.

He slipped off his own scuffed boots and put on the spares, feeling a strange sense of comfort in the familiar weight and fit. But as he stood up, a sudden jolt ran through his legs – not quite pain, but... This is just tiredness. He shook it off, chalking it up to a long day and a poor night's sleep. The landing gear was fine; no issues there. Cyrus made a mental note to replace the spare batteries in his radio, though, as he hadn't checked them yet. Maybe that was what was giving him these... odd tingles.

Cyrus stepped out of the maintenance room and into the main control area, his eyes adjusting to the dim light. He walked over to the radio console, where a small LCD screen glowed softly. He keyed the microphone and began to speak into it, trying to sound more professional than he felt.

"Hello, this is the cable landing station. Can anyone hear me?"

The static that responded was just as he had expected – nothing. He tried again, this time switching to a different frequency, but the result was the same: silence.

Cyrus let out a slow breath and stood up straight, raking his hand through his hair. Okay, so the batteries in the radio must be dead after all. He made a mental note to check them as soon as he could, and then tried calling again, this time using his phone.

The screen flickered to life as the phone connected to the satellite link, but when Cyrus tried to dial out, the numbers refused to register. The display froze on the keypad layout, as if waiting for him to enter a code.

He stepped back from the console, feeling a shiver run down his spine despite the warm summer evening air. That's strange. But he knew what was likely going on – it was just another minor issue with the satellite link, one that would be easy enough to resolve once he got the right people involved.

Cyrus took a deep breath and tried to shake off the feeling of unease settling in his chest. He walked over to the small café area near the control room, where a vending machine hummed quietly in the corner. Maybe a snack would help him clear his head...

Cyrus stood at the counter of the café, staring blankly at the vending machine as it dispensed a bag of stale chips into the tray. He opened the pack and sniffed the contents, his stomach growling at the scent. The crunch of the chips as he popped one into his mouth was a familiar sound, but it did nothing to distract him from the nagging sense that something was wrong.

He tried to focus on the task at hand – getting the satellite link up and running again – but his mind kept drifting back to the strange feeling of being watched. It started when he first arrived, but he'd pushed it aside as mere paranoia. Now, however, it had morphed into a creeping sense of disorientation.

As he munched on the chips, Cyrus noticed that the intercom in the corner of the control room – which had been stuck on a loop with the same message for what felt like hours – suddenly flickered back to life. "Welcome to the OceanView Submarine Cable Landing Station... Welcome to the OceanView Submarine Cable..." The words hung in the air, identical to before, but something about it now sent a shiver down his spine.

He glanced down at his wristwatch, which had been running fast for weeks – four minutes ahead of actual time. Now, as he checked it for what felt like the hundredth time that night, the discrepancy seemed to be growing.

Cyrus's eyes locked onto his watch as the discrepancy between its time and his surroundings grew. The words from the intercom still hung in the air, but he'd tuned them out. He couldn't shake the feeling that the clock was broken, that the very fabric of time had come loose.

He pushed back from the console and stood up, his joints creaking softly as he stretched. The control room was a tomb, the only sound the hum of the equipment and the gentle hiss of the ventilation system. The air felt stale, heavy with the scent of ozone and burned plastic.

As he moved closer to the intercom, the words seemed to grow louder, more urgent. "Welcome to... Welcome to..." He raised his hand to touch the device, and just as his fingers made contact, the lights in the room began to strobe wildly.

The sudden change made Cyrus's heart jump into his throat. He stumbled backward, tripping over the edge of the console chair. His palms hit the cold floor hard, and he scrambled to get up, his breathing growing ragged. The lights died, plunging the room into darkness.

Cyrus froze, waiting for his eyes to adjust. But instead of a gradual dimming, there was an absolute absence of light. He strained to see, but it was like being underwater. The air felt thick and heavy, pressing down on him. And then, with no warning, the temperature plummeted, making the hairs on the back of his neck stand on end.

He was alone in the darkness, surrounded by silence.

Cyrus's eyes strained to see through the darkness, but it was like trying to breathe through a clogged pipe. He could feel the air thickening around him, making his skin prickle with sweat. The cold seeped in, crawling up his legs like a living thing.

He tried to stand, but his feet felt rooted to the spot. A faint hum vibrated through the soles of his shoes, making them thrum like a bass note. Cyrus's mind reeled, trying to piece together what was happening. He thought back to the days since he'd arrived at the station. The strange symbols on the console. The discrepancy in the weather records.

A memory clicked into place: the lawyer's words, months ago. "I want my affairs settled, Mr. Sandoval. You're a man of routine, I'm told." Routine. Cyrus remembered the will, the way the lawyer's eyes had flickered over the words before he'd signed off. Routine.

The hum grew louder, and Cyrus felt his body begin to shudder. He was on his feet now, stumbling towards the door. But as he reached for the handle, his hand closed around something smooth and metallic. A small key fob. The one he'd found on the floor weeks ago. The one with a number etched into it: 27.

Cyrus Sandoval's records were filed away with the others, stamped INACTIVE on the top sheet. The submarine cable landing station was still operational, its humming machinery a steady beat in the darkness. The weather records for that day were missing, but the discrepancy had been noted and would be looked into. Routine procedure.

The new watchman, Jenkins, clocked in at 0600 hours, exchanging pleasantries with the security guard as he took over the rounds. Cyrus Sandoval's name was scratched off the roster, his badge returned to storage. The station's schedule was unchanged: maintenance checks tomorrow at 0800, replacement parts ordered from the mainland. Jenkins made a mental note to review the cable's repair history.

As he walked the catwalks, Jenkins noticed the console was still open on Cyrus Sandoval's workstation, keys pressed in place, the intercom crackling with static. He cleared his throat and spoke into the mic, the words echoing back at him: "Station Alpha, this is Security. Station Alpha, this is Security."
