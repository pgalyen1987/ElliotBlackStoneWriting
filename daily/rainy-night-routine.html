Rainy Night Routine

September 27, 2026 · 2643 words

Beatrix Hollowell stepped off the mail boat onto the slick dock, her eyes adjusting to the faint hum of lanterns swaying on their hooks. She'd been on this route three times a month for five years, ferrying post and packages between the scattered islands during the storms that knocked out the power grid on the mainland. It was a quiet life, one she'd grown to appreciate after years spent in cramped city shops, keys clanging and arguments brewing over every misaligned door.

She pulled off her gloves, revealing calloused palms, and tucked them into her jacket pocket as she approached the small cargo hold where the boat's crew had left the packages for her to sort. The air inside was heavy with the scent of damp paper and saltwater. Beatrix slipped on her glasses, a pair of scratched wire-rims perched on the end of her nose, and began sorting through the stacks of envelopes and boxes.

Her eyes passed over the familiar labels – "Hollowell's Locksmithing" – as she worked. She'd set aside a few dozen packages for tomorrow morning, when the power would flicker back on, but tonight was just a routine delivery run, nothing more. Beatrix's fingers moved with practiced ease as she sorted the keys and papers into labeled envelopes.

She changed out of her sodden boots into the spare pair that hung from a hook in the corner, the ones she kept for rainy nights like this. The worn leather molded to her feet without complaint, a habituated fit that spoke more to their shared history than any conscious memory. Beatrix didn't think twice as she worked; it was just another part of the night's routine.

The lanterns swayed in the breeze, casting shadows on the walls as Beatrix continued sorting packages. A faint scratching noise came from outside – a stray dog maybe – but it blended into the general din of rain and wind. She didn't look up, absorbed in her work as the darkness outside seemed to press closer.

Beatrix's hands moved with practiced ease as she sorted the packages into categories, her mind numb from the repetition of the task. The darkness outside seemed to seep in through the gaps around the windows, making the dim light within feel like a fragile barrier against the storm.

As she worked, her gaze drifted to the main cabin's controls, where the breaker panel hung on the wall. The breaker switch was flipped to the "off" position, its indicator light extinguished along with the rest of the boat's systems. But one light remained – the soft glow from the navigation lamp on the dashboard.

Beatrix frowned, her thoughts momentarily snagging. It was a small anomaly in an otherwise routine maintenance check, but it didn't add up. She pushed off from the counter and walked over to investigate. The breaker panel was still dead, but the nav light remained stubbornly lit.

For a moment, she considered investigating further – checking for blown fuses or short circuits – but then a logical explanation occurred to her. The nav light had a redundant power source, one that kicked in automatically when the main power went out. It was a safety feature, designed to keep the boat's essential systems operational even during a blackout.

Beatrix let out a soft breath, dismissing the anomaly as a minor glitch. She returned to her task, focusing on the packages as the sound of rain lashed against the windows. The wind outside seemed to be picking up, but it was just another storm – one that would pass with the dawn.

The lanterns cast flickering shadows on the walls as she worked, their light dancing in time with the creaking of the boat's wooden frame. A low-pitched growl echoed from the darkness beyond the windows, steady and persistent, like a metronome marking out the beat of the storm. Every minute, on the minute: 4...5...6...

Beatrix's fingers moved with practiced ease, the sound of rain drumming against the windows like a relentless heartbeat. She stacked packages onto the counter, their labels and stamps glowing faintly in the lantern light. As she worked, memories began to surface – not just fragments, but entire scenes: her parents' old farmhouse, a summer spent chasing fireflies, a birthday cake with candles that wouldn't blow out.

She suppressed them with a mental shake. This was normal. She'd had these memories before, hadn't she? But they felt... new. Uninvited. What's wrong with me? she thought, a thread of unease weaving through her concentration.

As she worked, the creaking of the boat's wooden frame seemed to grow louder, as if it was trying to match the rhythm of her memories. The air inside the mailboat felt heavy, almost thick, like a dense fog pressing against the windows. Beatrix pushed the thought aside, attributing it to the storm outside.

She poured herself a cup from the thermos that had been left on the counter – an oddity, since she'd never seen anyone else around. But the warmth of the liquid was reassuring, a small comfort in the midst of this tempest. She took a sip, feeling the heat spread through her fingers. Must have forgotten to turn off the stove.

The low-pitched growl continued outside, steady and unchanging. Every minute, on the minute: 7...8...9... Beatrix's eyes felt gritty, her mind snagging on the unfamiliar memories that kept surfacing. She forced herself back into the present, focusing on the packages as she worked – but the past lingered, an unwelcome presence in the shadows of her mind.

As she worked on the packages, Beatrix's mind kept drifting back to the thermos and the memories it had unlocked. She tried to shake it off, focusing on the task at hand. But her fingers moved slower now, and her thoughts were tangled with threads of unease.

She glanced up at the clock, the numbers ticking steadily: 10...11...12... The storm outside seemed to be holding steady, its low rumble a constant presence in her ears. She wanted to call for assistance, or at least confirm that she was still connected to the mainland. But the phone on the wall remained stubbornly dark.

She checked the lines again, running her fingers over the wires to make sure they were secure. It was probably just a blown fuse, she thought. The wooden frame of the mail boat creaked and groaned around her, but it was just the old vessel settling into its rhythms.

Just as she was about to call out to herself – must be a short in the wiring – she remembered the emergency radio on her belt. It was a backup, in case of situations like this. But when she turned it on, the static was immediate and overwhelming.

The silence that followed was worse than any sound. Beatrix's eyes drifted back to the clock: 13...14... The storm rumbled on outside, its steady heartbeat a stark contrast to her own racing thoughts.

A chill seeped into the room, insidious as a whispered secret. It was just the cold of the storm outside, but she could feel it gathering in the corners of the wooden frame, making the shadows seem longer and darker. Beatrix's breath caught, and for a moment, she forgot what she was doing. The packages were still on the counter, but her attention had wandered back to the thermos – and the memories that lingered, like cold sweat on her skin.

Beatrix's gaze lingered on the thermos, and for an instant, she was back in that small apartment, walking towards the kitchen as her husband poured them each a cup of coffee. The memory wasn't just visual; it was tactile – the feel of the ceramic mug warming in her hands, the taste of the coffee still slightly bitter from being freshly brewed. This is ridiculous, she thought, shaking her head. It's been months since he...left. She pushed the memory aside, focusing on the task at hand.

But as she turned back to the packages, another scene slipped into place: her standing in front of a small cabin, watching a little girl ride a bike down a dirt path. The sun was shining, and the trees were a vibrant green – but it wasn't summer. The air felt crisp and cool, and Beatrix remembered the taste of damp earth on her lips. This is impossible, she thought, trying to grasp onto some rational explanation, but there was none.

She stood frozen, her eyes scanning the room as if searching for a hidden camera or a recording device. It wasn't the first time memories from another life had invaded her thoughts, but it was getting harder to shake them off. The packages on the counter seemed to be mocking her – she'd been hired to open them, but what if they were more than just packages? What if...Beatrix's train of thought derailed as a faint whine echoed through the air: "Outside...outside...". A dog barked at regular intervals, its tone unmistakable. Beatrix knew that sound all too well now.

The storm raged on outside, but it seemed to be getting closer, seeping into the small, weathered cabin like cold air through a crack in the floorboards. She felt it gathering in the corners of the room – not just in her mind, but in the very walls themselves. This is ridiculous, she thought again, but the words felt hollow. Beatrix's eyes drifted towards the bed, noticing that the blanket was folded in a specific way, precisely the way she always folded it. But something about it seemed off – the creases were deeper, more deliberate. She shook her head, trying to clear the cobwebs, but the feeling persisted.

The packages on the counter beckoned, their labels still sealed and unopened. Beatrix's hand reached for them, her fingers hesitating as if sensing that whatever lay within was not something she should be opening. The barking outside continued its steady cadence – a reminder that time itself seemed to be unraveling, thread by thread.

Beatrix's hand hovered above the packages, her fingers trembling as if they'd developed a life of their own. The barking outside had grown louder, a cacophony that seemed to seep into every pore. She breathed in sharply, her lungs expanding with a sense of panic. The air in the room felt heavy, weighed down by the scent of damp earth and mold.

Her eyes darted towards the door, but it was still the same – the wooden frame battered but solid. Yet, the feeling of being trapped had never been so acute. Beatrix's gaze snapped back to the packages, her mind racing with the possibility that they were somehow connected to this growing sense of claustrophobia.

The ceramic mug on the counter seemed to stare at her, its glaze reflecting a faint image of the room outside – the trees swaying in unison with the barking dogs. Beatrix's skin crawled as she realized the mug was closer to the edge of the counter than it had been earlier. The packages were still sealed, but the labels now seemed to be twisted at an angle, as if someone had tried to rip them off.

A cold sweat broke out on her forehead as she felt a gentle pressure building in the room. It was like being underwater, where every movement made the water pressure change – a subtle shift that whispered its presence with every breath. Beatrix's lungs expanded again, this time with a sense of desperation. She tried to stand up, but her feet seemed rooted to the spot.

The barking had stopped.

For an instant, there was complete silence. Then, the sound of her own breathing filled the room – a steady, high-pitched whine that made her ears ache. Beatrix's eyes fixed on the packages, her mind racing with the certainty that whatever lay inside was now close enough to touch. The labels were twisted tighter, as if trying to contain something that wouldn't stay hidden any longer.

Beatrix's gaze darted to the door, her hand instinctively reaching for the lock on the wooden frame. But it was already open – a sliver of dim light slicing through the darkness, like a key turning in a lock. A figure stood just beyond the threshold, its features indistinct.

"Neighbor?" Beatrix ventured, trying to keep her voice steady.

The figure moved closer, and Beatrix's eyes adjusted to reveal Mrs. Finch, her usual kindly expression now twisted into something...off.

"It's time," Mrs. Finch said, her voice low and flat, devoid of its usual warmth. "You've done well."

Beatrix's mind reeled as she tried to comprehend the scene unfolding before her. The neighbor who always had a key. Who'd helped her through the power outage with her spare ceramic mug and warm soup. Mrs. Finch, who'd often stopped by for visits and left her own packages with little trinkets inside.

The labels on the packages now seemed to sear themselves into Beatrix's vision: "Fresh stock. Picked at 3:14 a.m. on the 31st day." The twisted threads, like they were trying to contain something that wouldn't stay hidden. This isn't possible. How many times had she used the phrase "my neighbor is so kind"?

Mrs. Finch's gaze flicked to the packages as if checking on some internal timer. Beatrix felt a shiver run down her spine, but it wasn't from fear – it was from recognition.

She'd produced something under duress before, and the thought sent a cold dread creeping up her spine. The smell of scorched wool wafted through her mind, followed by the taste of stale air on her tongue. Beatrix's eyes locked onto Mrs. Finch, who seemed to be waiting for...something.

The sound of Beatrix's own breathing filled the room once more, but this time it was laced with a growing sense of despair. She realized that she'd been waiting – not for rescue, or escape – but for the inevitable harvest.

Beatrix Hollowell woke up to the same grey, rain-slicked morning she'd left behind on the mail boat. The wooden frame of her bed creaked softly as she sat up, rubbing the stiffness from her neck. She lay back down for a moment, letting the quiet wash over her.

According to the schedule, Mrs. Finch's regular appointment was at 10:00 a.m. today. Beatrix made a mental note to review the records from last night's session; something was off about Mrs. Finch's behavior, and she couldn't quite put her finger on it.

She swung her legs over the side of the bed and planted her feet firmly on the floor. The ceramic mug from her midnight snack sat empty on the bedside table, a reminder that life went on despite the...other thing. She stood up, stretching her arms above her head, feeling the familiar ache in her lower back.

As she made her way to the kitchen, the hum of the coffee maker greeted her like an old friend. Beatrix poured herself a fresh cup and leaned against the counter, sipping the bitter brew. The morning sunlight streaming through the window highlighted the dust motes dancing in the air.

The routine was comforting – just another ordinary day. She glanced at the clock on the wall: 9:45 a.m. Still plenty of time to get settled before Mrs. Finch arrived.

Outside, the dog began its morning chant, barking three times, then falling silent for precisely thirty seconds before repeating itself once more. Beatrix smiled wryly; she'd grown accustomed to that rhythmic cadence over the years. It was a reassuring presence in an unpredictable world.

The coffee maker beeped, signaling the end of the brewing cycle. Beatrix moved to pour herself another cup, lost in thought as the morning wore on. The world outside her windows seemed oblivious to the events that had transpired last night – or, indeed, to the countless nights like it that had come before.
