Isolation Study

September 30, 2026 · 2327 words

Roland Quist scribbled numbers on a clipboard, his pencil hovering over the next column as he scanned the rocky outcropping for any sign of movement. The count was already running behind schedule, but the storm forecast hinted at clearer skies by nightfall. For now, the drizzle seeped into his skin, weighing down his hair like wet clay.

He worked at the Island Conservation Station, counting seabirds as part of a larger study on population trends. Roland's advisor had recommended him for the position, citing his background in ornithology and his willingness to take on the project alone for reduced compensation. The remote location was a boon for Roland; it meant no rent, no utilities, and a research stipend that barely covered expenses.

As he worked, the rain intensified, drumming against the metal roof of the station's main building. The sound was familiar now, a constant companion to his days on the island. Roland's gaze strayed to the stack of supplies by the doorway: crates of seeds, water containers, and a cache of emergency rations. He'd been warned about the isolation – no phone signal, no boats for weeks at a time – but he'd chosen it, calculating that the savings would pay off his student loans faster.

Roland refocused on the task at hand, scanning the rocks with practiced eyes for any sign of life. A nearby gull stirred, and he hastily recorded its presence. As he folded the clipboard to fit into his backpack, a corner of his jacket caught his eye – a pocket seam had come undone, and a piece of paper poked out. He pulled it free, smoothing the creases before tucking it back into his collar.

Roland rummaged through his backpack, searching for a spare pen to jot down some notes on the gull's behavior. As he dug deeper, his fingers brushed against a familiar folder – his notebook, where he kept track of his research progress. He pulled it out and flipped through the pages, stopping at the current month's entries.

On the last page, he noticed an unfamiliar scribble in the margin – a duty roster for the next month. His name was listed as the morning count supervisor. Roland's brow furrowed as he wondered who could have added this, but a likely explanation quickly presented itself: one of his colleagues must have misplaced their own notebook and grabbed his instead.

He recalled the previous day's conversation with Dr. Patel about the upcoming months' schedules. Someone must have jotted down a tentative plan, thinking it was their own notebook. Roland rubbed the crease on his forehead, trying to recall if anyone had mentioned next month specifically.

It was probably just an oversight – Dr. Patel would want to confirm the schedule with everyone before finalizing it. Roland didn't think much of it and refocused on his current task: finishing the day's count. As he scribbled down some final notes, a faint metallic tang tickled the back of his throat. He caught himself thinking about the coppery taste of pennies and shook off the sensation. It was just a side effect of eating too much processed food for lunch.

Roland stood at the edge of the research station's deck, binoculars slung around his neck as he scanned the island's west coast for seabird activity. The air was heavy with the smell of salt and the faint tang of decay that came with high tide. He spotted a flock of albatrosses wheeling in off the horizon and quickly scribbled down their numbers.

Back at the station, Roland began to sort through the day's notes, trying to make sense of the scattered impressions on his pad. Dr. Patel had stressed the importance of keeping meticulous records, and Roland was determined to deliver quality work for her grant. As he flipped through the pages, a faint crease caught his eye – not one he remembered making.

He leaned in closer, squinting at the page. The dates... they were correct, but the events listed... it didn't seem quite right. A blank space where he was supposed to have taken notes on the 14th of last month, but nothing else out of place. This is probably just a mistake I made during transcription.

Rolling his eyes, Roland refocused on the task at hand: entering the data into their shared spreadsheet. He tapped in the numbers with a familiar rhythm, but his gaze drifted back to the notebook. The blank page was still there, staring back at him like an accusation.

He told himself it was just a minor glitch – easily fixed with a simple update to the database. But as he looked up from the screen, Roland's eyes landed on something that made his heart skip a beat: a wristwatch on the edge of Patel's desk, its face glowing softly in the dim light. It read 9:17, but Roland knew it was only 8:13 outside. He'd checked the time just a minute ago...

Roland's fingers hovered over the phone on Patel's desk, hesitating between pressing call and scrolling through his emails. He needed to confirm the schedule for the day with their supervisor, but a glance at the time on the wristwatch only made him more confused. It was still 8:13 outside, but the watch said 9:17. A software glitch, maybe? He tried to shake off the feeling of unease, focusing on the task at hand.

The phone's cord snagged on the edge of the desk as he pulled it towards him, and Roland cursed under his breath. He yanked harder, but the cord remained stuck. He tried to free it, but it was as if the phone had developed a mind of its own, refusing to budge. Sweat beaded on his forehead as he tugged again, the sound of the phone's internal mechanism whirring in protest.

The air conditioning unit above the desk coughed out a burst of stale air, making Roland's skin prickle. He gave up on the phone and turned to the computer instead, hoping to check the schedule online. But when he opened his email client, he found that Patel had password-protected their shared account. The password was still set to "IslandTime" – a joke from their first week of work.

Roland's gut twisted as he tried the password again, but it refused to accept it. He checked the network connection, but it was stable. He even tried calling the station from his own phone, but the line went straight to voicemail. The research team's supervisor, Dr. Kumar, usually returned calls promptly, but Roland got only a brief beep before it cut off.

The silence in the room grew thicker as he stared at the phone, now stuck fast on Patel's desk. A faint hum from the air conditioning unit vibrated through his chest, making him feel trapped.

Roland's eyes drifted from the phone to the calendar on the wall. Eight days since he'd last heard from Kumar or Patel. That was impossible. He must have miscounted, but the math checked out in his head: 8:13 on the first day, and now it was almost 9:17. He had been trying to call, write emails, even send messages to Kumar's personal phone, but nothing. The station's satellite link was down, or so he thought, until he stumbled upon a page torn from the logbook.

Roland's hand shook as he touched the paper still adhering to the surface of Patel's desk. A fragment of writing remained: "Aug 12... three incidents... possible predation on juvenile birds..." The date was two days ago. He remembered nothing of August 12th, and his memory for events beyond the research station was sketchy at best.

Roland felt a wave wash over him as he read the words, like a cold breeze slipping under the door. It's not just the phone and the internet. His thoughts reeled as he tried to recall the past two weeks. No memories of writing in the logbook, no records of his own experiments or observations. The only thing that stood out was the increasing feeling of disorientation.

He stared at the torn page, his mind racing with scenarios. Had Patel or Kumar gone off-island without him? Was there something on this island he couldn't remember, something that was eating away at his mind?

The air conditioning unit rumbled to life, its hum growing louder, more insistent. Roland's chest felt heavy, as if the vibration had somehow seeped into his lungs. He took a deep breath and tried to shake off the creeping sense of dread.

But it stuck.

Roland's gaze settled on the calendar again, his eyes snagging on the scribbled notation: "8/13 - Departure?" He felt a cold trickle down the back of his throat.

The air conditioning unit's hum reached a screaming pitch, shattering the sudden stillness. Roland's skin prickled as the air around him began to vibrate. He felt it in his fingertips first, a buzzing that spread up his arms like pins and needles.

This is getting serious.

He stood, swaying slightly on legs that had grown leaden. The calendar's notation seemed to blur on the page, but he couldn't look away. Something outside the room was making the building shudder, the floor creaking beneath his feet.

Roland's gaze darted to the window, where a faint haze now clung to the glass like a gauze mask. The air inside felt heavier, almost palpable, as if the very walls were exhaling slowly into his lungs. He felt suffocated by it – trapped in this small space with some presence he couldn't quite see.

The air conditioning's shrill whine turned into a steady drone, its pitch dropping to something almost human. Roland's heart stumbled over its own beat as he recognized the sound: his own breathing, now loud and ragged, mingling with that of... something else.

A cold, damp draft swept through the room, rustling the papers on the desk behind him. The calendar page fluttered free, floating to the floor as if pulled by an unseen hand. Roland's gaze snapped down to it – the scribbled notation now read: "9:17 - Last count?"

Roland's gaze remained fixed on the calendar page, his mind struggling to grasp the altered notation. He tried to remember what he had written before – the 9:17 marker was correct, but what about the "Last count?" part? He racked his brain, attempting to recall each entry, each note, but they seemed...off. The dates blurred together, like pages torn from a book.

A faint buzzing in his ear made him shift uncomfortably in his chair. He had been trying to drown it out with the sound of the air conditioning, but now it was louder, more insistent. A vibration ran through his fingers as he touched his phone, which he'd placed on the desk earlier.

It wasn't there anymore. The space beside his hand was empty.

Panic flared up, and Roland's breath caught in his throat. He scrambled to his feet, frantically scanning the room for any sign of his belongings – phone, notebook, water bottle – but everything was gone. Only the faint glow of the computer screen remained, casting an faint light on the dust motes dancing in the air.

The calendar page still lay on the floor, its notation mocking him: "9:17 - Last count?" The words seemed to throb with a rhythm, like a heartbeat he couldn't ignore. Roland's mind recoiled from the implication – that this might not be his reality at all. That everything he thought was real, every moment of his research, every decision...might have been scripted.

He stumbled forward, hands outstretched as if grasping for something just beyond reach. His fingers brushed against an invisible surface, sending a jolt through his body. The air around him seemed to ripple and distort, like the surface of water when a stone is dropped.

Roland's eyes widened in horror as memories began to unravel – Patel's voice on the radio, Kumar's reassurances about the station's maintenance records, even the initial bird-count data...all these facts he'd taken for granted were now receding, like water draining from a tub. His own existence, once solid and sure, was dissolving into nothing more than...renderings.

He had been counting birds; he had been counting his life.

"Nine seventeen," Roland whispered, his voice barely audible over the whirring in his ear. "That's what it always says."

The weather log for the past twenty-four hours showed no unusual patterns: 7°C at dawn, rising to 10°C by mid-morning, with gentle breezes from the north-east. Roland's own pulse rate, taken at 9 a.m., was within normal parameters. The seabird count continued unabated, with forty-nine new arrivals noted during the morning. Patel's voice, recorded at 8:13 that morning, could be heard on the playback, discussing the pending delivery of replacement filters for the station's air purifiers.

Kumar's daily schedule, updated at 9 a.m., listed a routine inspection of the research station's roof at 10:00 a.m. The team had been making good progress on their data collection, and Dr. Patel was planning to send an email update to the research facility by the end of the day.

Roland himself had gone missing twenty minutes ago, after reporting for his scheduled task with the bird-census equipment. Kumar's search party was still combing the island, calling out his name, but there was no response. The sun was climbing higher in the sky, casting long shadows across the sand.

Back at the station, Dr. Patel was reviewing yesterday's bird-count records, noting that one of their team's observers had reported an unusual sighting: a bird with white feathers on its head, unlike any seen before on the island. She made a mental note to discuss this with Kumar and Roland during the afternoon briefing.

The clock on the wall ticked steadily on – 9:27, 9:28... As Dr. Patel scribbled some notes in the margins of her logbook, a metallic taste rose at the back of her throat, like pennies left too long in a glass of water.
