Empty Space Silence

September 23, 2026 · 2497 words

Ines Achebe poured herself a steaming cup from the thermos on her desk. She'd grabbed it on her way out of the office to grab lunch, but in the chaos of moving into her new apartment, she hadn't had a chance to drink it yet. As she sipped the bitter coffee, she gazed out the window at the deserted parking lot. The building's manager had assured her the previous tenants were all relocating for a new development across town, and Ines had jumped at the chance to snag the rent-controlled unit. She'd been meaning to move into a bigger space for months, but with her mom's surgery coming up, she'd put it off.

The air in the apartment was stale, but not unpleasantly so – just the kind of dryness that came from being empty. Ines had spent the morning unpacking boxes and organizing her new space, which was essentially a blank slate after the previous tenants' hasty departure. She worked through her lunch break, cataloging the various systems in the building for her company's database. Her fingers flew across the keyboard as she input the data on the air conditioning units, the boiler, and the security cameras – all of which, to her surprise, still functioned despite being unused.

The hum of the AC unit above her head provided a soothing background noise as Ines worked, and she'd already grown accustomed to the creaks and groans of the old building settling into its new emptiness. She'd even started to appreciate the faint silence that pervaded the deserted corridors, punctuated only by the occasional distant rumble of a truck driving by on the nearby highway. It was... almost peaceful.

Ines stood at the small kitchen table in her apartment, typing on her laptop as she went over the maintenance records for the building's heating system. The silence was still a novelty, but it had started to feel like just another part of the job: a quiet backdrop against which she worked. She'd almost forgotten what it was like to share walls with neighbors who complained about the noise or argued late into the night.

As she scrolled through the reports, her gaze fell on a small mark on the underside of the table. Ines's fingers paused mid-stroke as she leaned in for a closer look. The mark was a series of tiny teeth marks, the edges worn smooth by time and use. She wondered if it had been made by one of the previous tenants' pets.

Ines's mind offered up a plausible explanation: probably a dog that had gotten loose and chewed on something before its owner could catch it. It didn't make sense to overthink it; after all, plenty of buildings had pets running around without incident. She pushed the thought aside, returning her attention to the spreadsheets.

The rest of her afternoon flew by in a blur of calculations and data entry. When she finally put down her laptop, the sun had begun to set outside, casting long shadows across the empty rooms. Ines got up to grab a snack from the kitchen, her eyes passing over the table again as she went. The mark was still there, but it didn't seem quite so ominous in the fading light.

As she munched on an apple slice at the counter, Ines noticed something else: the fluorescent tube above the sink was ticking. It wasn't a loud or insistent noise, just a low, staccato beat that seemed to synchronize with her own pulse. She watched as it flickered once, then twice, before settling back into its steady hum.

Ines headed to the gym in her building, the usual four-minute walk taking forty minutes this time. She attributed it to the unfamiliar route she'd taken, trying to mix things up for her daily exercise. The apartment complex was faintly quiet, but that wasn't new – all the other tenants had moved out over a month ago.

She lugged her water bottle and towel into the dimly lit gym, the only sound being the creak of the old air conditioning unit above the treadmills. The fluorescent lights overhead seemed to hum in sync with the rhythmic thud of the AC. Ines started on the treadmill, setting the incline to a moderate level. As she warmed up, she noticed her feet felt heavier than usual, as if they were carrying an invisible weight.

Her routine was still intact – same playlist, same pace, same distance covered in thirty minutes. But something felt off, like the room had shifted slightly while she wasn't looking. She pushed aside the thought and focused on her breathing, trying to shake off the lingering fatigue.

After her workout, Ines headed back to her apartment, stopping at the coffee shop on the way for a quick pick-me-up. She grabbed a cup of black coffee from the counter, noticing a mug with a chip on the same side as hers – an odd coincidence that she didn't dwell on. At home, she took a moment to stretch on the couch, her eyes drifting toward the kitchen table where she'd left her notes and data screensavers.

The fluorescent tube above the sink was still ticking away in its staccato beat, somehow synchronized with her own pulse now. Ines shrugged it off as just another quirk of the old building, but a tiny frisson of unease tickled the back of her neck. She shook it off, telling herself she was just being paranoid – after all, everything still seemed normal on the surface...

Ines reached for her phone, which still clung stubbornly to 0% charge despite being plugged in all night. She muttered under her breath as she unplugged it and moved to grab the spare charger from its usual spot on the kitchen counter. Her fingers closed around an unfamiliar weight instead of the charger's cord.

She pulled out a small, silver pendant instead. It was her grandmother's – or rather, it had been passed down to her after her grandmother's passing. Ines didn't remember where she'd last seen it, but now it seemed to be nestled in the corner of the counter, surrounded by dust and the faint scent of old paper.

She shook her head, feeling a creeping sense of unease as she placed the pendant on its hook near the kitchen entrance. Maybe the charger was just misplaced? She glanced at the clock: 9:17 AM already, and still no sign of the building's maintenance crew to check on the electrical issues.

With a growing sense of frustration, Ines pushed her hair back from her face and headed for the living room. The thermostat display read 68°F – exactly where she'd set it before bed – but the air felt heavy and damp, like someone had just let the door slam shut behind them. She reached for her phone to check the building's emergency contact number, but as her fingers hovered over the screen, she realized it still wouldn't connect to the internet.

A tiny voice in the back of her mind whispered that maybe it was the router again – this old building's network had been wonky since the move. But Ines's gut told her something else: a cold, dark feeling that began seeping into her chest like frost on a winter night. She frowned, trying to shake off the sensation as she headed for the door...

Ines's footsteps echoed off the empty hallways as she made her way back to her apartment. Four minutes had passed since she'd last been there, but she could swear it took longer this time. The flickering fluorescent tube overhead cast an faint glow on the walls, and for a moment, Ines wondered if she was imagining things.

This is just fatigue, she thought, shaking her head. Too much coffee, too little sleep.

But when she opened the door to her apartment, she felt it – a growing sense of disorientation that had nothing to do with exhaustion. She'd taken four minutes to get here, but now it seemed like an eternity.

She tried to walk around the room, but her feet felt heavy, as if stuck in place. Every step took an effort, and her breathing quickened in response. Panic, she thought, trying to calm herself down. It's just a side effect of stress.

But it wasn't stress. It was something else.

She reached for the familiar shape of her keys on the hook by the door and paused. Something was off about them. A small key, one she hadn't used in months, lay nestled among the others. What was it for? She couldn't remember.

A spark of unease flared to life inside her, but she tried to brush it aside. It's just a misplaced key. Yet, as she turned the key over in her hand, the faint scent of dust and old metal rose up from its surface. A scent that seemed to belong to nothing she knew.

Ines's heart beat faster as she slid the key back onto the hook, trying to shake off the feeling of growing unease. But it was no use. The disorientation, the sensation of being lost in a familiar place – it was spreading. And it had nowhere to go but up.

Ines's hand closed around the doorknob as she pulled herself toward the living room. The air seemed to thicken, like a dense fog had settled over the space in seconds. She felt the sweat prick at her temples, and her breath caught in her throat. Nine seventeen, the same time every day – but now it felt suffocating.

She pushed the door open, and a faint hiss escaped from the hallway. A sigh of air released as she stepped into the room, followed by the creak of old hinges. Ines's eyes scanned the space: the couch, the coffee table, the wall of books – everything was where it should be. But something was off.

The temperature had shifted, rising in increments until her skin felt tight and hot. The air clung to her like a damp shroud. She reached out to touch the couch, and her fingers encountered an odd texture: the cushions were no longer plush, but rough, like sandpaper under her fingertips.

Ines's gaze landed on something else – a change in the room's layout. The bookshelf had been rearranged, books now stacked haphazardly, spines jutting out at awkward angles. A faint scent of wet clay clung to the shelves, overpowering the familiar tang of old paper and dust.

Her own breathing sounded loud in her ears as she took a step back, eyes darting around the room for any other signs of...of what? Something was watching her from just beyond the edge of perception. Ines's skin crawled with every slow tick of the clock on the wall, the seconds crawling by like hours.

She tried to swallow, but her throat felt parched and dry. The sound of her own heart pulsed in her ears, a steady drumbeat that underscored the silence. A faint scuffing noise came from the hallway – the creak of old wooden floorboards beneath some unseen weight. Ines's eyes locked onto the doorframe as it began to shift, ever so slightly, like a living thing adjusting its posture.

The doorframe shuddered, a tremor running through the frame like a pulse. Ines's gaze snapped to the hallway, where the creaking had grown louder, a rhythmic thud-thud-thud that seemed to match the beat of her own heart. She tried to move towards it, but her feet felt heavy, rooted to the spot.

The clock on the wall ticked on, each second stretching out like rubber. 9:18. 9:19. Ines's breath came in short gasps now, her lungs burning as she strained to hear what was making the noise. It sounded...familiar. Mechanic, repetitive, like a...

And then it stopped.

The creaking ceased, the thudding silenced. The hallway stood still, the air thick with an oppressive quiet. Ines's eyes darted around the room again, searching for anything out of place. That was when she saw them: small, unobtrusive labels on every surface, each one bearing a single digit – 1, 2, 3 – in neat, black ink.

The numbers seemed to leap off the walls, and Ines's mind reeled as she stumbled towards the kitchen. More labels, this time with two digits: 12, 14, 16. What did it mean? She yanked open a cabinet, revealing a stack of identical, plain boxes, each one stamped with an inventory number.

Ines felt her knees buckle beneath her. Boxes...inventory numbers...she remembered the union rep's conversation now – "just a little shift swap, Ines, nothing to worry about" – and his name etched into her mind like acid: Jenkins.

She backed away from the cabinet, horror spreading through her chest like ice. The labels, the boxes...it was all so...methodical. Routine.

The fluorescent tubes over the kitchen counter ticked once more, then flickered into life at 9:20. Ines's phone lay silent on the counter, still charging from the previous night. She hadn't bothered to charge her laptop or turn on the computer; there was no point now. The labels, the boxes, the inventory numbers – it all just seemed so...organized.

In the kitchen, a new calendar had been tacked up next to the old one. It was blank for the rest of the month, except for a handwritten note in Jenkins's familiar scrawl: "Routine cleaning schedule: Tuesdays and Thursdays, 9-11 am." As if anyone would still be living here.

Ines stumbled through her morning routine, mechanically washing dishes from the night before and pouring herself a glass of stale tap water. The water had been turned off, but she couldn't recall when exactly. It was hard to keep track anymore.

The clock in the hallway still chimed out 9:20 on the hour, its mechanical heartbeat steady and unremarkable. The ticking fluorescent lights above the sink matched its rhythm perfectly – tick, flicker; tick, flicker. Ines watched it for a moment, mesmerized by the monotony of it all.

As she continued to pace, her gaze fell upon the stack of identical boxes in the kitchen cabinet. They had been stacked neatly, labels facing outwards. The inventory numbers seemed to dance across her vision: 12, 14, 16...what did they mean? She remembered the union rep's conversation now – "just a little shift swap" – and his name etched into her mind like acid.

Ines shook her head, trying to clear it. It was just routine, after all. People moved in and out of this building every day. The labels, the boxes...it was all just part of the process.

As she turned away from the cabinet, something caught her eye: a small notebook, half-hidden beneath the stack of plain boxes. A page had been torn out, leaving behind a faint crease. Ines's heart sank as she recognized the handwriting – it was Jenkins's, and it seemed he'd written something after all.

The fluorescent tube above the sink ticked once more, then flickered into life at 9:21.
